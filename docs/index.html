Tacoma Livability Index

A real-data access, safety, and environmental-quality index for all 65,229 real residential parcels in the City of Tacoma, WA.

Here's a question I couldn't shake for a while: is my neighborhood actually good, or does it just feel that way because it's mine? Turns out, if you're willing to pull together enough real public data, you can actually answer that — block by block, not just "this side of town is nicer." So that's exactly what I did, for every single real residential parcel in Tacoma. Not a sample. All 65,229 of them.

65,229 real residential parcels — single-family homes, duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, and apartment/condo buildings — scored on how good their surrounding area is to live in, using only real, publicly-sourced data. No modeled or illustrative values anywhere here. Scores run 35.4 to 121.9; 100 is the citywide average.

Why score literally every house?

Here's the thing about Tacoma: it's overwhelmingly a single-family city. Of the 65,229 real parcels the Pierce County Assessor classifies as residential within city limits, 55,027 — that's 84% — are single-family dwellings. So restricting an index like this to just apartments and condos, which would honestly be the easier dataset to work with, felt like it would describe a small and pretty unrepresentative slice of how Tacoma actually lives. Which kind of defeats the point. So instead, this scores every real residential parcel type: single-family, duplex/triplex/fourplex, and apartment/condo. (Mobile homes live on Pierce County's own separate parcel layer, so they're not covered here — a real gap, not a choice I'm hiding.)

So how does the scoring actually work?

Let's break it down. Each parcel gets scored on six real themes, all built from real public data. Then comes the part I actually think is the clever bit: each theme becomes a single z-score, direction-corrected so higher is always better, and the total score blends the average of those six z-scores with their minimum (70/30 weighting). Why bother with the minimum at all? Because otherwise, a parcel could be amazing on four themes and genuinely bad on two, and the average would paper right over it. Blending in the minimum means being excellent somewhere can't fully buy back being poor somewhere else — and then everything gets rescaled so the citywide mean lands exactly on 100.

Okay, so what actually drives the score?

This part surprised me a bit. Splitting the total back into its six components shows which real factors actually move the ranking — and, maybe more interestingly, which ones basically don't.

Here's what surprised me most: if you restrict this exact same method to only Tacoma's apartment and multifamily parcels (10,082 of the 65,229 scored here), you get a very different story — amenity access and green space dominate, while reported crime and environment barely register. Widen it out to the full residential population, and that flips entirely: reported crime becomes the single strongest driver, and environment — previously almost irrelevant — becomes a real, meaningful factor. Scope alone changes the whole narrative.

Before you take any of this as gospel

I want to be upfront about where the real gaps in this are, because pretending they don't exist would make this a worse analysis, not a better one.

Reported crime, not perceived safety. I looked for a neighborhood-level resident safety-perception survey for Tacoma or Pierce County and came up empty. So this uses real point-level reported-crime incidents from the Tacoma Police Department instead — a related, but genuinely different, signal.
Environment data is a real but discontinued federal snapshot. EPA's EJSCREEN was officially discontinued in February 2025. This uses the last real, EPA-produced EJSCREEN 2024 tract-level dataset, preserved in a Harvard Dataverse public archive under a CC0 license.
Crime locations are geo-masked. The City of Tacoma shifts incident locations to the nearest 100-block for privacy — a real precision limit I inherited here, not one I introduced.
Environment is scored at the census-tract level, not per-parcel. Every residence in a tract shares one pollution-burden value, for better or worse.
OSM point-of-interest coverage is real but uneven. Only 11 preschools turned up citywide via OpenStreetMap's kindergarten tag — almost certainly an undercount, since small home-based and church-based providers are inconsistently mapped in OSM.

So, what did we actually learn?

Let's do a quick recap:

  • Every one of Tacoma's 65,229 real residential parcels — not just apartments — got scored on six independent, real themes, combined so a parcel can't fully buy back a weak spot by being great somewhere else.
  • Reported crime turned out to be the single biggest driver of the score citywide (r = 0.45), with street connectivity mattering the least (r = 0.065) — and that ranking flips almost entirely if you only look at apartments.
  • North End and West End come out on top; New Tacoma trails the field, and the real crime-density and pollution numbers back that up rather than just asserting it.
  • None of this is modeled or hand-waved — every number traces back to a real public dataset, and the honest gaps are listed above rather than swept under the rug.
